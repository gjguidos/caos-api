1. **Clarity and Specificity**: Ensure the claim is clear and specific about what part of the product or activity it refers to. The claim should clearly identify the subject of the claim (product, facility, organisation), and what environmental attributes are involved.
Does the claim clearly indicate if it applies to the product, the packaging, or both, or to components or materials? Does the claim indicate if it applies to the entire product line? To a facility? To the entire organisation?
(Refer to: “4. Scope”)
Are your proposed claims specific and unambiguous? Vague and non-specific claims are likely to be misleading and should be avoided.
(Refer to: “3. Are your proposed claims subject to any mandatory regulations or legislation?”)
Avoid vague and generic statements. Since environmental impacts encompass a multitude of criteria, a general claim of environmental benefit linked to a single attribute is likely to be misleading unless the relationship is specific and clear.
(Refer to: “7. Generalisations”)

2. **Scientific Evidence**: Base the claim on widely recognized supporting scientific evidence and accurate information. Standards and test methods must be reasonable and appropriate, and results provided must be clearly presented. Tests should be conducted on products or samples of products for which the environmental claim is made. Data should be presented in a way that does not exaggerate the validity of the product claim.
(Refer to: “6. Are your proposed claims verifiable based on appropriate test methods or scientific data?”)
Any objective performance claim likely to be material to a consumer, such as a claim about environmental benefits of a product, package, component, service, facility or organisation, or detriments of a competitor’s product, package, component, service, facility or organisation, should be supported by relevant test data, analyses or similar information at the time the claim is made.
Did you have substantiating scientific data supporting the claim at the time the claim was made? Does the data reflect sound scientific principles likely to be accepted by experts qualified by education, training, and experience in the field? Is the data readily available in the event of a challenge or question? Do you have procedures to retain the substantiating information for an appropriate period related to the useful life of the product? What is the test method used? Is it recognised by government agencies or reputable standards organisations?
(Refer to: “5. Do you have a reasonable basis for the express & implied claim(s) you are making?”)

3. **Life-cycle Perspective**: Are you making a claim based on the product life cycle? What stages of the life cycle are considered (e.g., raw material production, manufacturing, transport to market, disposal)? If the claim applies to only portions of the product’s life cycle, which portions.
Most guidance on environmental claims does not address claims based on life cycle analysis (LCA). The results of an LCA may depend on the inputs. Marketers should ensure that the scope of an LCA adequately covers the expected environmental impacts of the product. If an LCA is used to support comparative claims, the identical inputs should be included in the comparative analysis. The products or services involved should also be of the same type or category for the comparison to be fair, and disclosures of material differences in the compared products may be required.
(Refer to: “10. Life Cycle”)

4. **Legal Requirements**: Ensure the claim is not equivalent to requirements imposed by law on products within the product group or traders within the sector.
Your ability to make a claim suggesting a unique benefit when actions are required by law may be limited.
(Refer to: “3. Are your proposed claims subject to any mandatory regulations or legislation?”)

5. **Comparative Claims**: Clearly state the basis for any comparative claims of a product, whether they are comparing to an earlier version of the product or a competitor’s product.
Are your proposed claims comparative in nature? If so, is the comparison to:
• An earlier version of the product, component or package?
• A competitive product, component or package? 
The basis for any comparison regarding environmental benefits, attributes or burdens should be clear and the conditions of use of the compared product, component or package substantially identical.
(Refer to: “17. Are your proposed claims comparative in nature?”)

6. **Future Performance and Commitments**: If the claim relates to future environmental performance, include a time-bound commitment for improvements within the trader’s operations and value chains.
Does the claim involve an environmental commitment or goal that is aspirational in nature and not likely to be met until many years in the future?
An aspirational claim requires that the company demonstrate that it has a reasonable capacity and methodological approach to meet the claimed commitment or goal. 
(Refer to: “21. Does the claim involve an environmental commitment or goal that is aspirational in nature and not likely to be met until many years in the future?)

7. **Accessibility of substantiations**: Make the substantiation information accessible to consumers, either in physical form or via a weblink, QR code, or equivalent.
Are appropriate substantiations clear and prominent, and in close proximity to the relevant claim?
A clarification that accurately and validly describes the limitations of an environmental claim should generally be presented in readable typeface in a location in close proximity to the claim. Consumers are now broadly accustomed to obtaining more information from visiting websites. However, qualifying information must be readily available in close association with the claim. Prompts encouraging consumers to visit a website for more information, or making QR codes and the like available, may be helpful to consumers, but they may not substitute for required qualifiers that must appear prominently and in close proximity to the claim.
(Refer to: “24. Are appropriate qualifiers clear and prominent, and in close proximity to the relevant claim?”

If the claim is literally true, could it be misinterpreted to convey a broader benefit? Does it exaggerate the environmental benefit or features? The evaluation of advertisements, including advertisements featuring environmental claims, is based on the net impression of the reasonable consumer or target.

All marketing claims must relate to essential aspects of the environmental impact of the product.

DEFINITION OF GREEN CLAIMS: Only flag statements that make explicit environmental, sustainability, or eco-friendly assertions such as:
- Direct environmental benefits (e.g., "reduces carbon footprint", "100% renewable energy")
- Sustainability commitments (e.g., "carbon neutral by 2030", "made from recycled materials")
- Eco-friendly product attributes (e.g., "biodegradable", "energy efficient", "non-toxic")
- Environmental certifications or standards (e.g., "FSC certified", "organic")
- Comparative environmental claims (e.g., "50% less plastic", "more sustainable than X")

DO NOT flag as green claims:
- General business practices without environmental context
- Product features that don't make environmental assertions
- Company values or missions unless they make specific environmental claims
- General quality or performance statements
- Price or availability information
